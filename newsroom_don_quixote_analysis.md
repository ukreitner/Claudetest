## Don Quixote References in the Episode

### 1. Mac's Quote (Falsely Attributed to Cervantes)

**Mac's Speech:**
> "Now I'd like you to listen to these words which were written 500 years ago by Don Miguel de Cervantes: 'Hear me now, O thou bleak and unbearable world. Thou art base and debauched as can be. But a knight with his banners all bravely unfurled now hurls down his gauntlet to thee!' That was Don Quixote."

**Will's Correction:**
> "Those words were written 45 years ago by the lyricist for 'Man of La Mancha.'"

**Analysis:**
- Mac incorrectly attributes this quote to Miguel de Cervantes' original "Don Quixote" (1605/1615)
- Will correctly identifies it as being from "Man of La Mancha" (1965 musical)
- The lyrics were written by **Joe Darion** for the musical "Man of La Mancha"
- Music was composed by Mitch Leigh
- This is NOT from Cervantes' original work

### 2. The Epitaph Quote

**Quote Used in Episode:**
> "Here lies a gentleman bold / Who was so very brave / He went to lengths untold, / And on the brink of the grave / Death had on him no hold. / By the world he set small store— / He frightened it to the core— / Yet somehow, by Fate's plan, / Though he'd lived a crazy man, / When he died he was sane once more."

**Source:**
- This IS actually from Cervantes' "Don Quixote"
- Found in **Part 2, Chapter 74** (the final chapter)
- Written as an epitaph by the character Samson Carrasco for Don Quixote's tomb

## The Actual Cervantes Epitaph

### Original Spanish
```
Yace aquí el hidalgo fuerte
que a tanto extremo llegó
de valiente, que se advierte
que la muerte no triunfó
de su vida con su muerte.
Tuvo a todo el mundo en poco,
fue el espantajo y el coco
del mundo, en tal coyuntura,
que acreditó su ventura
morir cuerdo y vivir loco.
```

### English Translation (Various Versions)

**Literal Translation:**
> Here lies the valiant gentleman / Who to such extremes arrived / Of bravery, that it's noted / That death did not triumph / Over his life with his death. / He held the whole world in little regard, / He was the scarecrow and bogeyman / Of the world, in such a juncture, / That he credited his fortune / To die sane and live mad.

**Edith Grossman Translation (Modern, 2003):**
The final couplet:
> "for it was his great good fortune / to live a madman, and die sane."

**Traditional English Translation:**
> "Here lies a gentleman bold / Who was so very brave / He went to lengths untold, / And on the brink of the gave / Death had on him no hold. / By the world he set small store— / He frightened it to the core— / Yet somehow, by Fate's plan, / Though he'd lived a crazy man, / When he died he was sane once more."

### Key Phrase
The most famous line from this epitaph is:
- **Spanish:** "morir cuerdo y vivir loco"
- **English:** "to die sane and live mad" / "to die wise and live as a madman"

## The Don Quixote Theme in The Newsroom

### Character Parallels
- **Charlie Skinner** identifies with Don Quixote - "an old man with dementia who thought he could save the world from an epidemic of incivility simply by acting like a knight"
- **Will McAvoy** is compared to Don Quixote's horse/donkey
- **MacKenzie McKay** casts herself as Don Quixote in her conversation with Will

### "The Greater Fool" Concept
The episode title "The Greater Fool" references:
- An economic term for someone who buys overvalued assets
- In the show's context: someone naive enough to believe in "real news" and journalistic integrity
- Directly parallels Don Quixote - considered crazy for his idealistic quest

### Charlie's Quote (from elsewhere in the series)
> "I'm Don Quixote, you can be Sancho, she'll be Dulcinea, and everyone out there is the horse."
> **Will:** "Donkey. How did you know about that conversation?"

## Notable Translations of Don Quixote
- **John Ormsby** (1885) - Archaic but closest to original
- **Edith Grossman** (2003) - Modern, highly acclaimed for accuracy
- **Samuel Putnam** (1949) - Classic 20th century version
- **Tobias Smollett** (1755) - Historic translation

## Aaron Sorkin's Use of Don Quixote
Aaron Sorkin has expressed admiration for Don Quixote and uses the character as a metaphor throughout The Newsroom for idealistic journalists fighting against cynicism and corporate interests, knowing they may appear foolish but believing in the nobility of their quest.
